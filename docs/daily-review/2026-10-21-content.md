## The nasal ñ (P-092)

**Fixed tonight:** the course writes ں (noon ghunna) as ñ, but the future and
subjunctive had slipped to plain n in four sentences (s-19 "jaaoon", s-99 and
s-120 "karoon", s-213 "paṛhoon") and, found while fixing them, five grammar
notes; and لیموں was "laimoon". "laiñḍing", for the English "landing", had the
opposite slip. A test now holds the rule in the direction that is true: every
word spelt with ں has ñ in its Roman, except میں (main, "I", 34 sentences),
ہیں (hain) and ہوں (hoon), kept as learners write them. Every "main" in the
course is "I"; no "meñ" is mis-written.

**The other direction is not a rule.** 44 words carry ñ with no ں (aañkh,
chaañd, paañch, neeñd): a nasal vowel inside a word is spelt with an ordinary
ن, and `translit.ts` defines ñ as nasalisation, not as ں. They are right.

**Held for a native speaker (P-092b):** 21 words with a medial nasal vowel
written plain n, against the course's own majority (44 to 21): saanp, ṭaang,
gend, phenkna, oonchaai, ḍhoonḍna, bhaanji, moonchh, qainchi, kaanṭa (twice),
ghoonṭ, jheenga, goonj, baingan, chyoonṭi, jaanch, ḍaanṭe, hansna, pahunchna
(and s-222). Hindi spells all of them with a nasal mark (साँप, गेंद, हँसना);
some Pakistani speakers say a real n. Words with a true consonant n stay
(khaandaan, insaan, andar, jaanna). The laimoon vowel ("lemooñ"?) needs the
same ear.

**Against changing anything:** Pakistani Roman Urdu never writes ñ, and every
changed form is churn for Roman-track learners. Typed answers are unaffected
either way: "laimoon" still matches "laimooñ".
